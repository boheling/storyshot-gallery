# Who was the flag for? Explorers and flags, for 5th graders

**Request:** Make an explainer video for US 5th graders about flags in the age of exploration,
built around one question: who was the flag actually for? Start with Columbus pushing a flag into a
beach where people already lived, explain what the letters F and Y on it stood for, then show how
Spain, Portugal, France and England each marked land they said was theirs — and end on the fact
that every one of those places already had a name of its own. Everyday words and real comparisons
instead of units, and don't soften what happened.

Asked on 7 November 2026.

- **Type:** `explainer` (`purpose: explainer`, Storyshot 0.9.0), 16:9 1080p30, 2:37 (156.6 s),
  9 chapters. Nothing is recorded and there is no `url` in the storyboard: all 8 steps are
  [HyperFrames](https://github.com/heygen-com/hyperframes) scenes the agent wrote.
- **Voice:** Kokoro `af_heart`, free and local.
- **Sound:** Storyshot's own synth soundtrack, `calm` at 64 bpm.
- **Captions:** none on the picture. `subtitles.srt` and `chapters.txt` only.
- **Cost:** none beyond the agent itself. Local voice, local render, synth music, no API calls.

## Files

## The correction the film is built on

The request as it was first put had three errors in it, and they were worth getting right rather
than working around:

- The letters on Columbus's banner are **F and Y**, not Y and K. **F** is *Fernando* (Ferdinand II
  of Aragon) and **Y** is *Ysabel*, the fifteenth-century Spanish spelling of Isabel. No source
  gives a K; the likeliest explanation is an ornate drawn **F** read as a K somewhere in a
  classroom handout.
- Two letters because Spain in 1492 was **two crowns joined by a marriage**, not one kingdom. That
  is the fact that makes the detail worth a whole scene instead of a footnote.
- It is **Spanish, and 1492** — not English, and not 1600. England's claims come a century later
  and use different objects entirely.

Corrected, the story is better than the one that was asked for: Spain plants cloth, Portugal
plants stone, France plants a cross, England arrives almost a hundred years late and stays only
because of a crop. The film says so.

## What makes it the 5th-grade cut

There is a twelve-scene version of this film for adults. This is not that film shortened; the
places it differs are the point.

- **Every unit became a drawn comparison.** "A cross thirty feet high" became "taller than five
  grown-ups standing on each other" — and the scene stacks five figures beside the cross, with the
  cross a little taller. "370 leagues west of Cape Verde" became one line down the middle of the
  ocean, drawn once.
- **Named doctrines are gone.** No Requerimiento, no *Inter caetera*, no *Johnson v. M'Intosh*.
  The behaviour they describe stays: a man reading a document to people who cannot understand it is
  perfectly clear to a ten-year-old without its Latin name.
- **At most two or three pieces of text on screen at once**, titles at 86–96 px and labels never
  below 32 px, because this gets watched on a classroom projector.
- **Cartier's lie is kept**, because a ten-year-old understands a lie exactly. "It's only a
  marker." is struck through in red and replaced with "It was a claim on their land."
- **Nobody is caricatured.** Donnacona and the people on the beach are drawn with the same builder,
  the same ink and the same size as the explorers.

## Where it refuses to round off

- The banner's exact design survives only through later depictions, so every drawing of it carries
  a **reconstruction** mark on screen.
- "370 leagues" is said with **no mileage conversion**: sources convert the league to anywhere from
  ~1,100 to ~1,277 statute miles, and the film does not pick one.
- The film shows the **crosses** at Cape Henry, which the records support, and not the
  often-repeated Union flag at Jamestown in 1607, for which the only source found was a flag
  retailer's blog with no primary citation.

## The ending

Every place in the film already had a name. The last scene sets three of them large —
**Guanahaní**, **Stadacona**, **Tsenacommacah** — lets the ceremony strike all three out in seal
red, and then takes the strikes back off, drops the imposed names out of the frame, and brings the
three names back to full ink on the final words: *"But we still know them. And now you do too."*

A rule is still drawing under each name at the last frame. Nothing is held.

## Assessment

The thing this film taught its author is in the title. "Could a 5th grader follow this?" is a test
that cannot be passed by hedging — an expert audience lets vague phrasing through and a ten-year-old
does not. Forced to replace "thirty feet high" with "taller than five grown-ups standing on each
other", the sentence did not just get simpler; it got closer to what the thing actually was to the
people standing in front of it. Two beats from this cut were ported *back* into the adult film
afterwards.
